How to find your way

How a compass works

A compass needle is a small magnet. It swings until it lines up with Earth's magnetic field where you're standing, so one end points toward magnetic north.

Magnetic north isn't the same as true north, the direction of the geographic North Pole that maps are drawn to. The angle between the two is called declination.

Declination changes from place to place, sometimes noticeably over a mile or two. It also drifts over the years, because Earth's magnetic field is slowly changing. So a compass reading needs a correction that depends on where you are, and when.

How coordinates work

Latitude measures how far north or south of the equator you are: 0° at the equator, rising to 90° at each pole. Its lines, called parallels, run east to west.

Longitude measures how far east or west of the prime meridian you are. That's the line through the Royal Observatory in Greenwich, chosen as 0° in 1884. It runs to 180° east and 180° west, which meet on the far side of the world. Its lines, called meridians, run from pole to pole.

Each degree splits into 60 minutes (′), and each minute into 60 seconds (″). Decimal degrees write the same thing as one number.

Other ways to navigate

The stars

Polaris, the North Star, sits almost directly above the North Pole, so the other stars seem to wheel around it while it barely moves. The two stars at the end of the Big Dipper's cup point to it. Its height above the horizon gives your latitude: about 60° up means about 60° north.

The southern sky has no bright pole star. Navigators there use the Southern Cross to find due south.

The sun and shadows

At midday, when the sun is highest, a stick's shadow points north in the northern middle latitudes and south in the southern ones. Near the equator it can point either way, depending on the season. Sun time isn't quite clock time, because a clock keeps one time across a whole time zone.

The longitude problem

Latitude can be read from the sky, but longitude needs time. Earth turns 15° an hour, so each hour between local noon and the time at Greenwich is 15° of longitude. At sea, that needed a clock that kept time on a rolling ship.

Britain's Longitude Act of 1714 offered a reward for a solution. John Harrison, a self-taught carpenter, built a series of sea clocks, H1 to H4. H4 was tested on voyages to Jamaica in 1761 and Barbados in 1764, and Parliament later rewarded him, though not as fully as he felt he was owed.

Polynesian wayfinding

Pacific navigators cross open ocean without instruments. The core idea is the star compass: stars rise and set at known points on the horizon. When the sky is hidden, navigators feel the ocean swells, read clouds piled up over islands, and watch seabirds that fly out to sea in the morning and home at dusk.

Nainoa Thompson, taught by Mau Piailug of Satawal in Micronesia, navigated the canoe Hōkūleʻa to Tahiti without instruments in 1980.

Contour maps

A contour line joins points of the same height. Where the lines crowd together the ground is steep; where they spread out it's gentle. The lines across the top of this page are drawn the same way, from made-up terrain.

GPS

The Global Positioning System is owned by the United States and run by the U.S. Space Force. Its satellites broadcast their position and the time; a receiver picks up those signals and works out where it is, in three dimensions, and what time it is.

Animals

Many animals use Earth's magnetic field. Migrating birds have a magnetic compass; one leading idea is that a light-sensitive protein in the eye, cryptochrome, lets birds such as European robins sense the field. Exactly how it works is still mostly a mystery.

Young loggerhead sea turtles combine two features of the field, the angle of its lines and its strength, to judge where they are in the ocean, east to west as well as north to south.
